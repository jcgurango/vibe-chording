# Skeleton and Moves: A Catalogue of Chord Thinking

## Premise

A progression in this style is a slow skeleton of chords named against a home, with the gaps filled by moves named against a neighboring chord. Every example collected so far can be described with those two layers plus a few patterns that run across them.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Home | The chord that feels like rest. It can be major or minor, and it can shift for a few bars. |
| Skeletal chord | A chord analyzed against home: I, iv, ♭VI. |
| Move | A chord or short sequence analyzed against a neighbor. |
| Landing | The chord a move heads for. |
| Jumpoff | The chord a move leaves from. |
| Frame | What a chord is analyzed against: home, landing, or jumpoff. |
| Weight | How long a chord holds: a full slot, or passing through. |

Three points keep the definitions consistent:

- **Frame and weight are separate.** Most home-framed chords hold a full slot and most moves pass quickly, but a move can be stretched to a full slot. That is a promoted move: still framed by its landing, now carrying skeletal weight.
- **A chord can carry both labels.** E7 in C is "III7" against home and "dominant of Am7" against its landing. The label that explains more is the one worth showing.
- **A move is named for the landing it promises.** When the chord that follows is a different one, the move still keeps its name, and the mismatch is a deceptive landing.

Home itself nests. A move into IV is a move; stay on IV for two bars and it becomes a local home. A move, a tonicization, and a key change differ only in how long the landing holds.

## Skeletal chords

A major home has about 18 skeletal chords: seven diatonic, six borrowed from the parallel minor, and five borrowed from other parallel modes. Chord names below use C as home.

### Diatonic

| Numeral | In C | How it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Imaj7 | Cmaj7 | Home. It can go anywhere, and it is reached from V7, IV, iv, ♭VI, or ♭VII. |
| ii7 | Dm7 | Soft subdominant and a common starting chord. Leads to V7 or steps up to iii. Near-twin of IV. |
| iii7 | Em7 | A lighter stand-in for I. Falls to vi, or steps to IV or ii. |
| IVmaj7 | Fmaj7 | Subdominant and a common starting chord. Goes to V7, back to I, or turns minor (iv). |
| V7 | G7 | Dominant of home. Resolves to I, or deceptively to vi. |
| vi7 | Am7 | The relative minor and a second possible home. Near-twin of I. A common starting chord. |
| viiø7 | Bø7 | Rarely a resting chord. It works as the ii of vi: Bø7, E7, Am7. |

### Borrowed from the parallel minor

| Numeral | In C | How it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| i7 | Cm7 | Home turned minor for a moment. Opens the way to the other borrowed chords. |
| iiø7 | Dø7 | The minor-key ii. Pairs with iv and leads to V7. |
| ♭IIImaj7 | E♭maj7 | Goes to IV, ♭VI, or iv. The first chord of ♭III, IV, I. |
| iv7 | Fm7 | Minor plagal. Usually follows IV and resolves to I, with A♭ falling to G. |
| ♭VImaj7 | A♭maj7 | Near-twin of iv, and it shares C and G with home. The first chord of ♭VI, ♭VII, I. |
| ♭VII7 | B♭7 or B♭9 | The backdoor dominant. Resolves up a whole step to I. |

### Borrowed from other parallel modes

| Numeral | In C | Source | How it fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| v7 | Gm7 | Mixolydian | The ii of IV. Pairs with I7 to point at IV. |
| ♭VIImaj7 | B♭maj7 | Dorian, Mixolydian | The IV of IV. A softer ♭VII with no dominant pull. |
| ♭IImaj7 | D♭maj7 | Phrygian | Sits a half step above home. Slides down to I or slips around ii. |
| ♯ivø7 | F♯ø7 | Lydian | Descends by half step to IV, or works as the ii of iii. |
| IV7 | F7 | Dorian, blues | The blues IV. Returns to I or drops a half step to V7 of vi. |

### A minor home

A minor home has its own short list: i, iiø, ♭III, iv, V7, ♭VI, ♭VII. With A minor as home that is Am7, Bø7, Cmaj7, Dm7, E7, Fmaj7, G7. These are C major's diatonic chords with E7 in place of Em7.

This matters because many skeletons in the examples sit on the relative minor. Fmaj7, E7, Am7 looks unusual from C (IV, V of vi, vi). From A minor it is ♭VI, V, i, which is ordinary.

### How the borrowed major 7ths relate

The major 7th chords available against C line up as a chain of fourths:

G – **C** – F – B♭ – E♭ – A♭ – D♭

Each step to the right belongs to a parallel mode one shade darker, from Lydian (Gmaj7) down to Phrygian (D♭maj7), and adds roughly one note outside C major. Every chord on the chain either contains C or accepts it as a color tone. Major 7ths off the chain, such as Dmaj7 or Amaj7, contain C♯, so they contradict home and sound like a key change.

## Moves

A move is a recipe: give it a landing (and sometimes a jumpoff) and it produces the chords. There are ten moves that add chords, five variations that decorate a chord already in place, and three patterns that run across both layers.

### Moves framed by the landing

These need only the chord that follows, so they work on any landing, borrowed chords included.

| Move | Recipe | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dominant | The V7 of the landing | E7 → Am7 |
| ii–V | The related ii, then the V7. Into a minor landing: iiø, then V7♭9. | Bø7, E7 → Am7 |
| Tritone sub | A dominant 7th a half step above | B♭7 → Am7 |
| Slide | The same quality, or a major 7th, a half step above or below | B♭maj7 → Am7 |
| Diminished | A diminished 7th a half step below. It is the landing's V7♭9 without its root. | G♯dim7 → Am7 |
| Backdoor | A dominant 7th a whole step below. Strongest into a major landing. | B♭9 → Cmaj7 |
| Plagal | The IV or iv of the landing. The minor form needs a major landing. | Fm7 → Cmaj7 |

### Moves framed by both neighbors

| Move | Recipe | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diatonic walk | The scale chords stepping from jumpoff to landing | Cmaj7 (Dm7, Em7) Fmaj7 |
| Chromatic passing | The chord between two roots a whole step apart | Em7 (E♭dim7) Dm7 |
| Pre-echo | The landing's near-twin, played just before it | Dm/F → Fmaj7 |

### Variations on a chord already in place

These are framed by the jumpoff. They change the chord being left and add no new one.

| Variation | What changes | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality flip | The chord turns dominant or minor before leaving | Am7 (A7) Dm7; Fmaj7 (Fm7) Cmaj7 |
| Bass walk | The harmony holds while the bass steps toward the landing through inversions | E7 (E7/F♯, E/G♯) Am7 |
| Sus delay | The sus4 sounds first and resolves inside the slot | E7sus4, E7 → Am7 |
| Line cliché | One inner voice moves by half step while the chord holds | C (Caug, C6, C7) Fmaj7 |
| Side-slip | A half step away and straight back | Dm7 (D♭maj7) Dm7 |

### Patterns across both layers

**Deceptive landing.** A dominant-type move can land on the near-twin of the chord it promised. E7 promises Am7 and can land on Cmaj7 or Fmaj7, which share most of Am7's notes. The move keeps its name and both landings are recorded.

**Planing.** One chord quality moves across several roots. It is a way of choosing chords, so it appears at both levels. At skeletal weight it selects borrowed chords (a run of major 7ths). At move weight it is the slide and the side-slip. Planing that sounds good meets three conditions:

1. Each chord keeps the home note alive, as a chord tone or a color tone.
2. Each chord belongs to a parallel mode of home, which for major 7ths means staying on the chain of fourths.
3. The roots form a pattern the ear can follow: a scale, a pentatonic, or a chain of one interval.

**Combination.** Moves stack, chain, and stretch.

- Stacking: two moves or variations share one gap, such as a quality flip plus a bass walk.
- Chaining: a dominant can itself be a landing, which gives runs like E7, A7, D7, G7.
- Promotion: any move stretched to a full slot takes skeletal weight.

### Fall in Love Alone

The skeleton is plain: B♭maj7, E♭maj7, Gm7, Cm7. Each gap uses a different move, which makes this the clearest single example of the two layers.

- Into E♭maj7: Cm7 and Dm7 are a diatonic walk up from B♭maj7.
- Into Gm7: D7 is the dominant. D7sus4/E and D/F♯ are a bass walk, D to E to F♯ to G.
- Into Cm7: Gm7 flips to G7, then Am7 and G/B walk the bass G to A to B to C.
- Into B♭maj7: E♭maj7 is plagal and A♭9 is the backdoor, stacked.

### Pick You Up

Em7, E♭maj7, Dm7, Gm7 reads most simply with D minor as home, though the song's key is unconfirmed.

- Dm7 is i and Gm7 is iv.
- E♭maj7 is ♭II, and it also acts as a slide into Dm7 held for a full slot.
- Em7 starts a chromatic descent in the bass, E to E♭ to D.

### Scott and Zelda

Gm7, C9, Am7, D7♭9 is ii, V, iii, VI7 with F as home, and F never arrives.

- Gm7 and C9 form a ii–V that promises F.
- Am7 and D7♭9 form a ii–V into Gm7, landing when the loop wraps around.
- Both pairs hold full slots, so the loop is made of promoted moves.
- Bridge: B♭maj7, Am7, A♭, Gm7 is IV, iii, ♭III, ii. The A♭ is a chromatic passing chord between Am7 and Gm7. It sits where D7♭9 sits in the verse, and it is D7's tritone sub.

### Fmaj7, E7, Am7, Gm7 (C7)

This is the Just the Two of Us shape.

- From C: IV, V of vi, vi. From A minor: ♭VI, V, i.
- E7 is a dominant into Am7, promoted.
- Gm7 and C7 are a ii–V whose landing is Fmaj7 at the top of the loop. Gm7 is also v7 from the palette.

### Horoyoi

A♭maj7, G7, Cm7, E♭7 is the same shape in E♭.

- G7 is a dominant into Cm7, promoted.
- E♭7 is I7, a dominant into A♭maj7 when the loop wraps. It is the last example's ii–V with the ii left out.

### Odoriko

I, V7 of vi, vi, IV, with iv taking the second half of the last bar.

- V7 of vi is a promoted dominant.
- IV to iv is a quality flip. The result is a minor plagal move into I at the wrap.

### House of Gold

F, A, Dm, B♭m is Odoriko compressed.

- A is the dominant of Dm, promoted, played as a plain triad.
- B♭m is iv at full weight. It skips the IV and resolves to F at the wrap.

### favorite

Three sections, each closer to D minor than the last.

- Verse: F, Dm7, Gm, Am is I, vi, ii, iii. All diatonic.
- Prechorus: Gm7, C, Am7, Dm7 is ii, V, iii, vi. The second pass adds G/D, Am7, Dm7. G/D reads best as a decoration of Dm7, with the D held in the bass under a brighter chord, but this is the least certain reading here.
- Chorus: Gm7, A7, Dm7 is ii, V of vi, vi from F. From D minor it is iv, V, i.
- Gm7, G♭maj7, Gm7 is a side-slip.
- Am7 between Gm7 and Dm7 is a one-chord diatonic walk.

### Hvnly

G♭, A♭, B♭m, Fm is IV, V, vi, iii in D♭, or ♭VI, ♭VII, i, v in B♭ minor. The three passes differ only in the chord squeezed before G♭ returns, and each is a pre-echo.

- B♭m shares two of its three notes with G♭.
- E♭m over a G♭ bass is nearly G♭maj7 already. The chord was noted as E♭m/G, which is probably E♭m/G♭.
- E♭m in root position is the same idea with the bass a third lower.

### loser

Em, A, D, Bm is ii, V, I, vi in D. It is all skeleton with no moves, and it is the baseline the other examples decorate.

### tourguide

D, E, Amaj7, F♯ is IV, V, I, VI in A.

- F♯ major is the dominant of Bm (ii), held for a full slot.
- The loop wraps to D instead. D is Bm's near-twin, so this is a deceptive landing.

### Teach Me Slow

The chorus is functional and the other sections are planed.

- Chorus: Cmaj7, E7, Am7, Fmaj7 is the Odoriko skeleton without the iv. The tail, B♭maj7 then A♭maj7, is ♭VII then ♭VI. A♭maj7 is the near-twin of Fm7, so it resolves to C the way a minor plagal does.
- Verse: Cmaj7, B♭maj7, Cmaj7, E♭maj7 is I, ♭VII, I, ♭III. The passing Fm7 is a minor plagal move back to Cmaj7.
- Prechorus: A♭maj7, B♭maj7 lands first on Fmaj7, then on Cmaj7 for the chorus. The same pair is ♭III, IV, I with F as a local home, then ♭VI, ♭VII, I in C.
- Planing: every chord outside the chorus is a major 7th. The roots C, E♭, F, A♭, B♭ spell C minor pentatonic and form a chain of fourths. All three planing conditions are met.
- The chorus leans sharp (E7 adds G♯) and the other sections lean flat, which is why the chorus brightens when it arrives.

### Cmaj7, B7, G, E7♭9

Both dominants land on the near-twin of what they promise.

- B7 promises Em and lands on G.
- E7♭9 promises Am and lands on Cmaj7 at the wrap.
- Bmaj7 between B7 and G is a quality flip that cancels the pull to Em. Every voice then slides a half step into G.
- The second E7 voicing, over B, is a bass walk into C.
- The loop never plays a minor chord although both dominants imply one.

### The two diminished examples

- V, vii° of vi, vi: G7, G♯dim7, Am7 in C. This is the diminished move, with the bass rising G to G♯ to A.
- Bdim, Ddim, Fdim, G♯dim into Am7: the four chords are one diminished 7th in its four inversions. This is the same diminished move stretched by a bass walk that climbs in minor thirds and ends a half step below the landing.

### Two cadences worth naming

♭VI, ♭VII, I and ♭III, IV, I are the same pair of whole-step major chords with two different landings. The pair can land a step above its second chord or a fourth below it. Teach Me Slow uses both within one prechorus.

## Scoring dimensions

A candidate for a slot, whether a skeletal chord or a move, is judged on eight dimensions. The first three say how well it connects, the next three how well it suits the slot, and the last two how well it suits the progression so far. All numbers in this section are starting guesses to be tuned by ear.

| Dimension | Question it answers | How it is measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Right fit | How strongly does it point at the next chord? | For a skeletal chord, the strength of that pairing as a common motion. For a move, the strength of its recipe. |
| Left fit | Does it follow naturally from the previous chord? | The strength of the pairing, or smoothness when the pairing is not a known one. |
| Smoothness | How little do the voices have to move? | Shared notes and total step distance against both neighbors. |
| Tension fit | Is it as tense as this slot can take? | The chord's tension level against the slot's length and beat position. |
| Room | Does it fit in the time available? | The number of chords in the move against the length of the gap. |
| Distance from home | How far outside the key does it reach? | Notes outside the home scale, or steps along the chain of fourths. |
| Pattern | Does it continue a pattern already under way? | Same quality as recent chords, with a root that extends their sequence. |
| Variety | Has this just been used? | Repeats of a neighbor, or of a move used in the previous gap. |

### Notes on each dimension

**Right fit counts most.** Most moves are defined by their landing, and the last slot of a loop has the first chord as its right neighbor. When only the left neighbor is known, left fit takes over.

**Smoothness covers what the catalogue has not listed.** Fmaj7 to E7 is not a textbook pairing, but the two chords share a note and the rest move by step, so the pair scores well anyway. Unlisted pairings are then ranked by how they sound.

**Tension fit links the chord to the rhythm.** A chord lasting half a beat before a downbeat can be far tenser than one lasting a bar. Long slots on strong beats favor low levels and short slots before a downbeat favor high ones.

| Tension level | Chords |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Diatonic |
| 1 | Borrowed |
| 2 | Dominants aimed at something other than home |
| 3 | Diminished chords and tritone subs |

**Room keeps length honest.** A two-chord move needs space for two chords. A longer move earns nothing for being longer.

**Distance from home is a taste setting.** The same distance that is too far for one song is the point of another. It works best as a single dial that shifts the preference toward or away from home.

**Pattern is how planing gets in.** When the last two skeletal chords share a quality and their roots follow a scale or a chain, a candidate that continues the sequence scores higher. The three planing conditions decide how much.

**Variety is a small penalty.** Loops repeat by design, so it applies only to immediate repeats.

### How strongly each move points

| Move | Strength |
| --- | --- |
| Dominant, ii–V | 1.0 |
| Tritone sub, diminished | 0.8 |
| Diatonic walk, chromatic passing | 0.7 |
| Backdoor | 0.7 into a major landing, 0.4 into a minor one |
| Plagal, slide | 0.6 |
| Pre-echo | 0.5 |

A deceptive landing scores the move against the landing it promised, then discounts it to about 0.6 of that strength.

### Sequences and variations

A multi-chord move is scored as one candidate. Right fit comes from its last chord, left fit from its first, and the links inside it are guaranteed by the recipe.

Variations do not compete with chords, because they decorate a chord already chosen. They are judged on smoothness and room alone.

### Relative importance

A reasonable first weighting is right fit 3, left fit 2, smoothness 2, and 1 each for the rest.

One caution applies to any single ranking. The safest diatonic chord will usually score highest, so candidates are better compared within their own kind: stable chords against stable chords, borrowed color against borrowed color, and moves into the next chord against each other.

## Voice leading

A chord is voiced as a shape one hand could play, and the next chord takes the nearest such shape. Treating each note as a separate voice that finds its own nearest chord tone is the wrong model, because nothing then holds the voices together and they spread apart.

### Two layers

| Layer | What it plays | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bass | The root, or the slash note when there is one | Its own low register |
| Upper shape | Three or four notes in close position | A fixed window around middle C, about G3 to G5 |

The two are decided separately. A slash chord such as D/F♯ changes the bass layer only. Rearranging the upper shape is a different decision, and it never sounds like an inversion because the root is still at the bottom of the whole sound.

### What goes in the shape

- The 3rd and 7th are always present. They define the chord, and they usually hold or move by step.
- The root can be left out because the bass has it. The 9th takes its place.
- The 5th can be left out. The 13th takes its place.
- The top note is heard as a melody, so it should hold or move smoothly.

### Choosing the next shape

1. Take the chord's notes after any swaps. A rootless Dm9 is F, A, C, E.
2. Stack them within an octave. Four notes give four rotations, and each can sit at any octave that fits the window, for roughly 8 to 12 candidates.
3. Cost each candidate against the previous shape.
4. Take the cheapest.

| Cost term | What it measures |
| --- | --- |
| Movement | Total semitones moved, matching voices low to high. This is the main term. |
| Pull toward center | Distance of the shape's middle from a home point near middle C. It stops the slow drift that movement alone allows. |
| Top note | Extra cost for a leap in the highest voice. |
| Bad shapes | A minor 9th between two voices, two voices on one note, or close intervals low in the window. |

Because every candidate spans less than an octave, a shape can never spread out. The pull toward center corrects register gradually, and a deliberate jump is saved for section boundaries.

### Extensions as a result

A 9th or 13th is often what the voice leading produced, with the chord symbol written afterwards. There are three common ways this happens.

- **A held note.** E on top is the 3rd of Cmaj7, the 7th of Fmaj7, the 9th of Dm9, and the 13th of G13.
- **A shape reused over a new bass.** Fmaj7 over D is Dm9. Em7 over C is Cmaj9. Am7 over F is Fmaj9.
- **A passing half step on a dominant.** The ♭9 of D7♭9 is E♭ on its way down to D, the 5th of Gm7.

The 9th on major and minor chords is also simply the default sound of this style, chosen on purpose. Voice leading then decides which rotation is played.

### Wider voicings

Spread voicings exist, but they are a deliberate choice. Dropping the second-highest note of a close shape down an octave gives the drop 2 voicing of guitar and ballad piano. On records the spread usually comes from the arrangement: bass low, keys in the middle, and voice or strings on top, with each part compact.

### How this connects to the rest

- The smoothness dimension in scoring is this movement cost, measured between a candidate and its neighbors.
- A bass walk is a change in the bass layer while the upper shape holds.
- A line cliché is one voice of the upper shape moving by half step while the others hold.
- The one-hand limit is also what keeps the result sounding played. A chord nobody could hold is not a candidate.
